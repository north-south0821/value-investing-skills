# Case Study: GE Preferred Stock (2008)

> **"Only when the tide goes out do you discover who's been swimming naked."**
> — Warren Buffett, 1993 Shareholder Letter (perfectly validated in 2008)

## Background

### Macro Environment (September–October 2008)
- Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy on September 15, 2008 — the largest bankruptcy in US history
- The global financial system was on the brink of collapse; credit markets were frozen
- US Treasury and Congress scrambled to negotiate TARP (Troubled Asset Relief Program)
- The VIX fear index hit all-time highs
- Almost no one would lend to any financial institution — regardless of fundamentals

### GE's Situation
GE wasn't an ordinary company:
- America's largest industrial company, market cap once exceeded $600 billion
- Businesses spanned aviation engines, energy, healthcare equipment, and finance (GE Capital)
- The only remaining original Dow Jones component from the index's 1896 founding

But in 2008, the problem was:
- GE Capital (the finance arm) was enormous and held substantial real-estate-related assets
- Frozen credit markets meant GE Capital couldn't issue commercial paper (CP) to fund operations
- GE faced a liquidity crisis — not because operations were bad, but because of systemic financial panic

> GE's problem wasn't "can we repay?" — it was "we can't borrow to bridge the gap."

## Buffett's Investment Thesis

### 1. This Wasn't "Buying Cheap" — It Was a Structured Deal
Buffett didn't simply buy GE common stock. He engineered a **preferred stock + warrants** structure:

**Berkshire purchased $3 billion of GE perpetual preferred stock:**
- Annual dividend rate: **10%** ($300 million per year)
- Redemption clause: GE could redeem after 3 years at 110% of face value
- **Attached warrants:** Right to buy $3 billion of GE common stock at $22.25/share within 5 years

### 2. Why This Structure Was Brilliant

| Return Source | Detail |
|--------------|--------|
| **Dividend income** | $300M/year (10% current yield) — pays regardless of stock price |
| **Redemption premium** | If GE redeems after 3 years, Berkshire gets $3.3B ($3B principal + $300M premium) |
| **Warrant appreciation** | If GE stock recovers above $22.25, warrants generate significant profit |
| **Downside protection** | Preferred stock ranks ahead of common in capital structure — GE must pay preferred dividends first |

**Core logic:** This wasn't "I think GE stock will go up" speculation — it was a structure designed to **profit regardless of what the market does**.

- GE recovers → Buffett participates in upside via warrants
- GE doesn't recover but can pay dividends → Buffett earns 10% annually
- GE goes bankrupt (lowest-probability scenario) → preferred ranks ahead of common in claims

### 3. Buffett's Brand Advantage = Deal Advantage
In 2008, when everyone refused to lend, only Berkshire had the cash and reputation to provide this capital. GE's CEO Jeff Immelt **called Buffett** — because he knew only Buffett had sufficient cash and conviction to act in a crisis.

> **"When everyone was looking for the exit, Buffett was the entrance."**

This created an irreplicable competitive advantage:
- When ordinary people panic, they sell
- When Buffett panics, he **negotiates** — because he holds what everyone desperately needs: **cash**

## Key Numbers

| Metric | Data |

| Investment amount | $3 billion |

| Annual dividend rate | 10% |
| Annual dividend income | $300 million |
| Warrant strike price | $22.25/share |

| GE stock price (Oct 2008) | ~$20-22 |

## Results

### Near-Term Development (2009–2011)

| Date | Event |
|------|-------|
| Oct 2008 | Buffett invests $3B, market views it as a "vote of confidence" in GE |
| 2009 | GE slashes common dividend (from $0.31 to $0.10/quarter), but preferred dividend is paid as contracted |
| 2009-2010 | GE Capital gradually shrinks and reduces risk exposure |
| 2011 | GE redeems preferred stock for $3.3 billion ($3B principal + 10% premium) |

### Final Return

- 3-year holding period, **deterministic returns:**
  - Dividend income: 3 years × $300M = $900 million
  - Redemption premium: $300 million
  - Warrant value: GE stock recovered to ~$20 by 2011 (didn't exceed strike price), warrant value was limited, but Buffett could hold until 2013
- Conservative estimated total return: **~$1.2 billion** (3 years on $3B principal)
- Annualized return: approximately **12-15%** — during a financial crisis!

And this was just the GE deal. Add similar structured deals with Goldman Sachs, Bank of America, etc. — Berkshire harvested massive "structural profits" throughout the financial crisis.

## Key Lessons

### 1. Cash Is the Ultimate Call Option
Before 2008, many criticized Buffett for "holding too much cash, missing the bull market." When the crisis hit, that cash delivered double value:
- Avoided being forced to sell assets during panic
- Enabled negotiating exceptional terms for structured deals

> **"Cash is trash in a bubble and king in a panic."**

### 2. Don't Just Buy — Negotiate
Ordinary investors can only "accept the price the market gives." But when you're well-capitalized and well-reputed, you can **create deals** rather than accept them.

Application: Even as an individual investor, you can place "ridiculously low" limit orders during extreme panics — sometimes they get filled.

### 3. Protect the Downside, Let the Upside Take Care of Itself
The 10% preferred dividend provided a solid downside floor. Even if GE's stock never rose, Buffett still earned 12-15% annualized. For ordinary investors: pay attention to dividend yields. Even when the stock price wobbles, dividends pay you to wait.

### 4. Systemic Panic ≠ Company Fundamentals Are Broken
GE's problem wasn't that nobody wanted its jet engines — it was that frozen financial markets prevented short-term funding. Systemic risk and company-specific fundamental risk are different things. In a panic, the market **doesn't distinguish** between them — everything finance-related gets sold. That's the value investor's opportunity.

### 5. Brand and Reputation Are Investment Tools
GE called Buffett, not someone else, for a reason — Buffett's "white knight" brand made him the first call in a crisis. This $3 billion preferred stock deal was likely negotiated in less than a weekend — because he didn't need "committee approval," and GE's management trusted him to deal fairly.

## FAQ

**Q: Ordinary people can't do preferred stock deals — what's the takeaway?**
A: The structural details are specific, but the principles are universal:
1. Have cash when markets panic
2. Seek downside protection (dividends, asset backing)
3. Systemic risk ≠ fundamental risk
4. In extreme moments, negotiate (limit orders, patience) rather than trade impulsively

**Q: What if you'd bought GE common stock instead of preferred in 2008?**
A: GE common stock performed terribly from 2008 to 2018 — falling from ~$30 to ~$7. Buffett's preferred structure **perfectly avoided** that loss. Instrument selection is itself part of investment competence.
