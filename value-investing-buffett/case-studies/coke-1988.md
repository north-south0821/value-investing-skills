# Case Study: Coca-Cola (1988)

> **"When I started selling Coca-Cola in 1935 or 1936... it was already a terrific business. But its growth had barely begun."**
> — Warren Buffett, 1998 Annual Meeting

## Background

### Company Status (1988)
- **Company:** The Coca-Cola Company
- **Industry:** Beverages (carbonated soft drinks)
- **Market Cap:** ~$15 billion
- **Brand History:** Founded 1886, 100+ years old
- **Global Presence:** Sold in 150+ countries, but international per-capita consumption was still very low

### Competitive Environment
- PepsiCo was pressing through the "Pepsi Challenge" taste tests and youth-focused marketing
- 1985 "New Coke" launch was a disastrous failure — consumer backlash forced restoration of the original formula after 79 days
- This failure ironically proved: Coca-Cola's brand power far exceeded the product itself

### Why Buffett Got Interested
After Black Monday (October 19, 1987 — Dow dropped 22.6% in a single day), markets panicked. Coke's stock fell from ~$40 to ~$35-38 alongside the broader market.

## Buffett's Investment Thesis

### 1. The World's Widest Consumer Brand Moat
> **"If you gave me $100 billion and said, 'Take away the soft drink leadership of Coca-Cola in the world,' I'd give it back to you and say it can't be done."** — Buffett

Moat sources (three layers):
- **Brand moat:** Coca-Cola isn't just a beverage — it's a piece of American culture. Globally, "cola" = Coca-Cola
- **Distribution network:** The world's most extensive beverage distribution system — every remote village shop carries Coke
- **Economies of scale:** Global volume creates unmatched unit cost advantages

### 2. Massive Growth Runway
1988 global per-capita Coca-Cola consumption:
- USA: ~300 servings/year
- China: <1 serving/year
- Global average: ~30 servings/year

Buffett's simple logic: as the global economy develops, worldwide per-capita consumption converges toward US levels. This was a **multi-decade** growth story.

### 3. Extremely High Return on Capital
Coca-Cola's business model:
- Produce concentrate → sell to bottlers → bottlers produce and distribute

Coca-Cola **doesn't bear the capital expenditure of bottling and distribution** — that's the bottlers' problem. Coke earns a light-asset "brand royalty."

This means:
- ROIC is extremely high (little reinvestment needed)
- Growth doesn't require massive capital support
- Profits earned are genuine free cash flow

### 4. Currency Tailwind
~80% of Coke's profits came from outside the US, mostly in local currencies. If the dollar weakened (Buffett believed US trade deficits were unsustainable), international profits would translate to more dollars.

## Key Numbers

| Metric | Data |
|--------|------|
| Initial investment | ~$1.02 billion |
| Initial stake | ~7% |
| Average purchase price | ~$10.96/share (split-adjusted) |
| Purchase P/E | ~13-15x (reasonable valuation at the time) |
| Purchase dividend yield | ~2.5% |
| 1988 global soft drink share | ~44% |

## Results

### 10-Year Mark (1998)
- Initial $1.02 billion → market value ~$13.4 billion
- **10-year return: ~13x, annualized ~29%**

### 35-Year Mark (2023)
- Berkshire still holds 400 million shares (worth ~$24 billion)
- Annual dividend: ~$736 million
- **Each year's dividend alone is ~70% of the original investment cost**

## Key Lessons

### 1. Crises Are the Investor's Friend
Black Monday 1987 created the buying window. Buffett didn't buy because he "predicted the crisis" — he bought because he'd done his research in advance and acted decisively when the crisis hit.

### 2. Brand Moat Investment Value
Coca-Cola's brand doesn't just generate profits today — more importantly, it makes **profits 10 years from now highly predictable**. You know people will still drink Coke in a decade. You're less sure they'll use any particular phone.

### 3. The Global Middle-Class Theme
A multi-decade macro trend — Buffett didn't "predict macro," but he spotted an ongoing trend and found the best beneficiary.

### 4. Light-Asset Model Value
Coca-Cola doesn't need to reinvest most of its profits. All profits are "real money you can spend" — and can be used to find more investment opportunities.

### 5. Don't Be Distracted by Short-Term Noise
The 1985 "New Coke" crisis, Pepsi taste-test challenges, health-conscious dietary concerns — all this short-term noise caused many investors to miss this investment. Buffett saw: 100 years of brand equity isn't overturned by one generation's taste trends.

---

> **Note:** Today (2020s), the global carbonated beverage consumption trend is different (health-conscious shift). The same company can be a completely different investment opportunity in different eras.
