# Case Study: The Washington Post (1973)

> **"In 1973-74, we bought The Washington Post at one-quarter of its intrinsic value. Not because we were smarter than others — because the market went insane."**
> — Warren Buffett

## Background

### Company Status (1973)
- **Company:** The Washington Post Company
- **Business:** Newspaper publishing (The Washington Post) + Newsweek magazine + 4 TV stations
- **Market Cap:** ~$80 million
- **Core Asset:** The Washington Post — Washington D.C.'s dominant newspaper, with enormous political and social influence

### Macro Environment
- **Oil crisis:** OPEC oil embargo sent prices soaring, economy entered stagflation
- **Stock market crash:** Dow fell from ~1,050 (early 1973) to ~578 (late 1974) — a 45% drop
- **Watergate:** The Washington Post was at the center of investigating Watergate, facing intense political pressure from the Nixon administration
- **Pessimism:** The market viewed newspapers as a "sunset industry"

### Why Buffett Got Interested
Buffett knew the newspaper industry deeply. He'd been a paperboy at 13 and later owned the Omaha Sun. He understood the newspaper economic model:

> **"In any city with two newspapers, eventually only one will survive. That surviving paper will possess unmatched pricing power."**

The Washington Post was exactly that — Washington D.C.'s dominant daily.

## Buffett's Investment Thesis

### 1. Franchise Value
Newspapers in that era were **information infrastructure**. No internet, no social media — people got news, job listings, and classified ads primarily through newspapers.

The Washington Post's franchise included:
- Washington D.C.'s largest daily newspaper — a **de facto monopoly**
- 4 TV stations (broadcast licenses were scarce, government-regulated)
- Newsweek magazine

### 2. Extreme Undervaluation (Margin of Safety Classic)

Buffett's sum-of-parts valuation:

| Valuation Method | Estimated Value |
|-----------------|-----------------|
| 4 TV stations' market value | ~$150 million |
| Newsweek magazine value | ~$50 million |
| Newspaper business value | At least $100-200 million |
| **Sum of parts** | **At least $300-400 million** |
| **Entire company's market cap** | **~$80 million** |

> **The market was quoting an insane price: you could buy $300-400 million of assets for $80 million.**

This equated to a margin of safety of **75-80%**.

### 3. Defensive Business Characteristics
Newspapers were **inflation-resistant** assets:
- Could raise prices (classified ads, subscriptions) without losing many customers
- Required little capital reinvestment (profits ≈ free cash flow)
- High fixed costs but low variable costs — margins expanded as revenue grew

In the stagflationary 1970s, these characteristics were especially precious.

### 4. Katharine Graham's Management
Katharine Graham was the publisher of The Washington Post. Buffett held her in the highest regard — she demonstrated courage defending press freedom under extreme pressure (during Watergate). Buffett became not just an investor but a personal friend and advisor, helping her understand corporate finance and capital allocation.

## Key Numbers

| Metric | Data |
|--------|------|
| Initial investment | ~$10.6 million |
| Initial stake | ~10% |
| Purchase P/E | ~3-4x (extreme undervaluation) |
| Purchase price vs. intrinsic value | ~25% (meaning 75% margin of safety) |
| Market cap at purchase | ~$80 million |

## Results

### Holding Journey
| Year | Event |
|------|-------|
| 1973 | Buffett begins buying, total investment $10.6M, 10% stake |
| 1974 | Watergate escalates, Nixon resigns. Post's reputation peaks |
| 1974-1980 | Company uses monopoly position to steadily raise prices, profits grow rapidly |
| 1980s | Company consistently buys back stock — well below intrinsic value |
| 1990s-2013 | Buffett holds for over **40 years** |
| 2013 | Washington Post sells newspaper business to Jeff Bezos |

### Returns
- Initial $10.6 million investment
- After 40 years, worth over **$1 billion**
- Annualized return: >**12%** (over 40 years!)
- **~1,000x return**

## Key Lessons

### 1. When Extremely Undervalued, Precision Isn't Needed
Buffett didn't build a DCF model. He did a simple sum-of-parts: what are the TV stations worth? (market comparables exist). The magazine? The newspaper? Add them up, divide by market cap → 75% discount. This rough calculation sufficed — because the margin was so large that even if the TV station valuation was wrong by half, he'd still make money.

### 2. Markets Can Be Absurdly Wrong
The Efficient Market Hypothesis claims prices reflect all information. The Washington Post case is the counter-proof: a company with $300-400 million in assets trading at $80 million. Markets don't just "sometimes err" — in extreme panic, they can be **absurdly wrong**.

### 3. Courage Comes from Understanding
Buffett's courage came from his deep understanding of the newspaper business. He knew the economics, knew the monopoly advantages, so he dared to act when the market panicked. **The ignorant need courage. Those who understand are just doing their job.**

### 4. Great Management Is a Plus, Not a Requirement
Most of the Washington Post's value came from the franchise (the moat), not management. But Katharine Graham's exceptional leadership (especially defending press freedom during Watergate) confirmed for Buffett that this company's values matched his.

### 5. True Long-Term Holding Spans Generations
40 years — longer than most careers. Buffett's patience allowed him to hold from the "golden age of newspapers" all the way to the internet disruption, exiting at the right time. He didn't sell prematurely but observed for years until the newspaper moat was definitively proven eroded.

## Modern Analogues

While the newspaper industry has been disrupted, the methodology still applies:
- March 2020 COVID panic: some quality REITs, hospitality, airline stocks traded at 30-40% of asset value
- 2022 China ADR crash: some quality internet companies traded below their net cash holdings

**When the market prices something clearly worth $4 at $1, you don't need an iPhone to see that fact.**
