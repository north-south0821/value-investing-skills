# Case Study: See's Candies (1972)

> **"See's Candies was the turning point of our investment career. It taught us that buying a wonderful business at a fair price is far better than buying a fair business at a wonderful price."**
> — Warren Buffett

## Background

### Company Status (1972)
- **Company:** See's Candies
- **Industry:** Premium boxed chocolates
- **Location:** California (primarily San Francisco and Los Angeles)
- **Founded:** 1921 (50 years operating history)
- **Revenue:** ~$30 million
- **Pre-tax Profit:** ~$4 million
- **Net Assets:** ~$8 million

### What Made See's Unique
- California consumers had deep **emotional attachment** to See's
- It was the go-to gift, especially during holidays (Christmas season represented a huge share of annual sales)
- See's had built a "quality promise" — consumers trusted it unconditionally
- Pricing was significantly above ordinary chocolate, and consumers happily paid the premium

> One consumer: **"If I come home without See's, my wife will know I'm having an affair."**
> This is the strongest possible evidence of a brand moat.

### Why Buffett Got Interested
This deal was driven by Munger, who knew See's as an iconic California brand. At the time, Buffett was still steeped in Graham's "cigar butt" approach — only wanting extremely cheap "lousy companies." Munger convinced him: this company was worth paying a premium for.

## Buffett's Investment Thesis

### 1. Brand Moat: The Purest Case
See's possessed textbook brand moat:
- **Emotional connection:** Consumers weren't just "buying chocolate" — they were "delivering love"
- **Holiday tradition:** Christmas + Valentine's Day = highly predictable, recurring demand
- **Price insensitivity:** See's buyers didn't care if prices rose
- **Regional density:** Dense store network in California created "everywhere" brand awareness

### 2. Pricing Power: The Ultimate Moat Proof
Buffett and Munger discovered See's could **regularly raise prices** without losing customers. This is the strongest proof of any consumer brand moat.

> Munger: **"Look at See's Candies: almost every year we could raise prices, and customers didn't mind at all. There are very few businesses where you can do that."**

Over subsequent decades, See's average annual price increases far exceeded inflation — and volumes not only held but grew.

### 3. Extremely High Return on Capital

| Metric | Data |
|--------|------|
| Net assets (1972) | ~$8 million |
| Pre-tax profit (1972) | ~$4 million |
| **ROE** | **~50%** |

A company needing only **$8 million in net assets to generate $4 million in pre-tax profit** — this is a monster on return on capital.

More importantly: **it didn't need to reinvest most of the $4 million to keep growing.** Earned profits were **genuine free cash flow**, available to invest elsewhere.

### 4. A Simple Business
Buffett could easily understand See's:
- Buy ingredients (chocolate, nuts, sugar)
- Make beautiful boxes of chocolate
- Sell through owned retail stores
- Consumers pay a premium due to emotional connection

No supply chain PhD needed, no engineering PhD needed, no semiconductor process understanding needed. A 12-year-old could grasp this business.

## Key Numbers

| Metric | Data |
|--------|------|
| Purchase price | $25 million |
| Net assets | ~$8 million (purchase price >3x book value) |
| Graham "cheap" standard | ❌ Fail (P/B > 1, not a cigar butt) |
| Buffett "fair price" standard | ✅ Pass (~12-13% after-tax initial return) |

By Graham's standards, 3x book value was **too expensive**. This made Buffett deeply uncomfortable at the time.

Why Munger's argument won:
- ROE was 50%, not 10%
- Profits were growing, not declining
- Pricing power meant earnings certainty was high

## Results

This is one of the greatest investments in Berkshire history.

From 1972 through the 2020s:

| Dimension | Data |
|-----------|------|
| Revenue (1972) | ~$30 million |
| Revenue (peak year) | >$500 million |
| Cumulative pre-tax profits | **>$2 billion** |
| Cumulative additional capital required | **<$30 million** |
| **Cumulative return vs. investment** | **>8,000x** |

The even more astonishing discovery: See's generated over $2 billion in pre-tax profits over 50+ years while requiring less than $30 million in additional capital to maintain and grow operations.

**All remaining profits were deployed by Berkshire into other investments** — like Coca-Cola, American Express. See's didn't just earn great returns itself; it provided the "ammunition" for Buffett to fight more battles.

This extends Buffett's "float" concept: a business that generates massive cash flow while consuming almost no capital is the ultimate compounding amplifier.

## Key Lessons

### 1. Buffett's "Paradigm Shift"
Before 1972 = Graham-style cigar butt investor (buy cheap, regardless of quality). After 1972 = Buffett 2.0 (buy wonderful companies at fair prices).

> **"Charlie dragged me from being an ape to being a human."** — Buffett

Without See's Candies, there would be no later Coca-Cola, Gillette, or American Express — all built on the "fair price for a great business" logic.

### 2. Brand Value Is Severely Underestimated
Most investors and analysts focus on **tangible assets**: factories, equipment, inventory, cash. They use P/B to measure cheap vs. expensive.

But See's most valuable assets were **intangible**: brand emotional connection, consumer trust, holiday traditions. These never appear on a balance sheet — yet they're worth more than any tangible asset.

> Buffett's conclusion: **"The moat is the most valuable asset you'll never see on a balance sheet."**

### 3. Pricing Power > Scale > Low Cost
See's had no scale (a regional California brand) and wasn't the lowest cost (premium positioning). But it had **pricing power** — which was its entire moat.

Investors often chase scale effects and cost advantages, but **pricing power is the highest form of moat** — because it reflects consumers' **emotional dependency** on the brand.

### 4. Simple Business + Deep Insight
See's business was simple enough for any first-year MBA student to analyze. But **simple ≠ easy to spot** — it required deep insight into consumer psychology ("if I don't bring See's, my wife knows I'm cheating"), not complex financial models.

### 5. The Essence of a Compounding Machine
See's = High ROIC + Low capital needs + Pricing power → Profits can be deployed into higher-value opportunities. This is the "compounding amplifier" Buffett constantly seeks:
- A business that makes money without needing money
- The earned money buys more such businesses
- Each cycle compounds larger than the last
