# 06 — Don't Predict the Market

> **"We've long felt that the only value of stock forecasters is to make fortune tellers look good."**
> — Warren Buffett, 1992 Shareholder Letter

## Core Idea

Buffett and Munger don't predict the macro economy, interest rates, or market direction. They have **no idea** where the stock market is heading over the next 12 months — and are proud of it.

Why? Because predicting markets is **impossible** — and more importantly, for true investors, it's **unnecessary**.

> **"In the short run, the market is a voting machine. In the long run, it's a weighing machine."** — Benjamin Graham

- **Voting machine** (short-term): Prices driven by emotion, fund flows, panic, greed. Unpredictable.
- **Weighing machine** (long-term): Prices converge to intrinsic value. Valuation skill determines returns.

## Why Market Prediction Is Futile

### 1. Nobody Can Do It Consistently

From Buffett's 1992 letter: "We will continue to ignore political and economic forecasts, which are an expensive distraction for many investors and businessmen. Thirty years ago, no one could foresee the massive expansion of the Vietnam War, wage and price controls, two oil shocks, the resignation of a president, the dissolution of the Soviet Union, the Dow's 508-point single-day crash..."

**Not a single person** successfully predicted any of these major events — and each one moved markets. Yet great companies, after all these events, **hit new highs**.

### 2. The "Two-Layer Uncertainty" of Macro Forecasting

Macro prediction requires getting two things right:
1. Correctly predict the macro variable (GDP, rates, inflation)
2. Correctly predict the market's reaction to that variable

Even if you nail #1 (already near-impossible), the market may move in the opposite direction for other reasons.

### 3. Predicting Correctly ≠ Investing Successfully

Some foresaw the 2008 housing bubble. They shorted — but got squeezed out before the eventual crash. **Timing and direction are equally important, and timing is nearly unpredictable.**

## What to Do Instead: Focus on Micro, Ignore Macro

| Macro (Unknowable) | Micro (Knowable) |
|--------------------|------------------|
| What will GDP growth be next year? | What has this company's ROE been for 5 years? |
| Will the Fed raise rates? | What's this company's competitive position in its industry? |
| How much longer will the bull market last? | How capable and honest is management? |
| Where are US-China relations heading? | How large a discount is the current price vs. intrinsic value? |

> **"Micro is what I can know. Macro is what I cannot. I spend my energy on the former."** — Munger's implicit logic

### Exploit the Market, Don't Predict It

"Mr. Market" is Graham's greatest contribution:

> Imagine you have a business partner named "Mr. Market." Every day he quotes a price — he'll buy your shares or sell you his at that price. Mr. Market is emotionally unstable. When happy, he sees only bright futures and quotes very high. When depressed, he sees only trouble and quotes very low. **You don't have to accept his quotes.** You can ignore him, or exploit him — buying when he quotes absurdly low, selling when he quotes crazily high.

## Buffett's "Market Timing" — Contrarian Investing

"Don't predict" doesn't mean "don't act during extremes." Buffett's actions were never based on "has the market bottomed?" predictions, but on an empirical observation: **when everyone panics, good companies' prices often fall far below value.**

| Event | Buffett's Action | Logic |
|-------|-----------------|-------|
| 1973-74 bear market | Heavily bought Washington Post | "Fearful when others are greedy" |
| 1987 Black Monday | Began buying Coca-Cola | Market panic, great company on sale |
| 2000 dot-com bubble | Refused to participate, held cash | "Greedy when others are fearful" (in reverse) |
| 2008 financial crisis | Invested in Goldman Sachs, GE (preferred stock) | Acted when Mr. Market was extremely depressed |
| March 2020 COVID | Sold some positions (later deemed too early) | Even Buffett makes mistakes |

**Key distinction:** Buffett didn't predict these crises — he researched the companies he wanted to buy **before** the crises, then acted when the crisis made prices attractive.

## How to Practice

1. **Block the noise** — Delete stock app push notifications. Reduce macro news consumption (once a week is enough). Unfollow short-term-prediction pundits.
2. **Build an "opportunity list"** — Before storms arrive (calm periods), research 10-20 companies you'd want to own. Write down: their moats, estimated intrinsic value range, price you'd buy at.
3. **Wait for storms (maintain cash reserves)** — Never go all in. Buffett always holds ample cash (Berkshire routinely keeps $200-300B+ in cash equivalents), waiting for market panic. **"Cash is a call option — it gives you the right to buy cheap in the future, with no expiration date."**
4. **Execute during panic** — When markets crash, news is all bad, everyone is selling — open your "opportunity list," check which have hit your buy price, and execute. No need to judge "has it bottomed." Just **cheap enough** will do.

## Common Misconceptions

- ❌ **"Don't predict" = don't be cautious at market highs** — During the 2000 dot-com bubble, Buffett faced "are you dinosaurs?" mockery and refused to buy at highs. He wasn't "predicting" a crash — he observed that **no company met his buy criteria**.
- ❌ **"Don't predict" = never sell** — When market price far exceeds intrinsic value, selling is rational.
- ❌ **Trying to predict "policy markets"** — Some investors love guessing what policy comes next. Buffett would say: if the company you own is worth 100, and the stock drops from 70 to 50 because of some policy — your job isn't to "guess the next policy." It's to **verify the company is still worth 100, then consider adding**.
- ❌ **Contrarianism = always doing the opposite of everyone** — Going against the crowd isn't an end in itself. If the crowd is right (e.g., avoiding a fraudulent company), you avoid it too — that's rationality.
