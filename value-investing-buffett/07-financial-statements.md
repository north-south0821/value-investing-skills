# 07 — Reading Financial Statements: The Language of Business

> **"Accounting is the language of business. If you don't understand that language, you cannot evaluate a business."**
> — Warren Buffett

## Core Idea

Buffett considers financial statement literacy an investor's **basic skill** — like a doctor reading lab reports, or a pilot reading the instrument panel.

But Buffett's approach differs from most investors:
- He's not "calculating" (like an accountant verifying figures)
- He's "**translating**" — converting accounting numbers into business narratives

> **"If you finish an annual report without understanding the company's economic reality better than when you started, you've wasted your time."** — Buffett

## Buffett's Four Layers of Financial Reading

### Layer 1: Earnings Quality (Not Quantity)

**Core question: Are the reported profits real cash profits?**

| Check | Warning Signal |
|-------|---------------|
| Receivables growing much faster than revenue | May be loosening credit terms to "manufacture" revenue |
| Inventory growing much faster than revenue | Products may be piling up unsold |
| Operating cash flow consistently below net income | Profits are "paper" profits, not turned into cash |
| High proportion of non-recurring items | Core profitability is weak after adjustments |

> **"I'd rather have a lumpy but real 15% return than a smooth but fake 12%."** — Buffett

### Layer 2: Return on Capital (ROIC, not just ROE)

Buffett doesn't just look at ROE — he cares more about **Return on Invested Capital (ROIC)**.

Why: ROE can be amplified by adding leverage (more debt) — which increases risk. Buffett asks: for every dollar of capital invested in this business, how much **after-tax, unlevered** return does it generate?

> High ROIC + sustainable = good business
> High ROE + high leverage = be careful

### Layer 3: Financial Evidence of the Moat

Use financial data to verify the moat:

| Evidence of Moat Existence | Early Warning of Moat Decline |
|---------------------------|------------------------------|
| 5-10 years of stable high gross margins | Gross margin declining 3+ consecutive years |
| CapEx / Net Income < 50% | CapEx rising but revenue not growing |
| Free Cash Flow / Net Income > 80% | FCF turning negative |
| Price increases faster than cost increases | Starting discounts/promotions to maintain share |

**The most crucial moat indicator: gross margin stability.** A truly moat-protected business has **stable and highest** gross margins in its industry. If gross margin begins a sustained decline, the moat is narrowing.

### Layer 4: How Management Treats Shareholders

Not in the three main statements — found in "Management Discussion & Analysis" and behavioral signals:

| Good Signal | Bad Signal |
|------------|-----------|
| Annual report candidly discusses mistakes and challenges | Annual report is a highlight reel of good news |
| Share buybacks done when actually undervalued | Buybacks at high valuations to boost executive options |
| Equity-based compensation is restrained | Excessive low-strike-price options (hidden dilution) |
| Conservative accounting policies | Aggressive accounting (early revenue recognition, expense capitalization) |

> **"We like managers who report bad news as promptly and in as much detail as good news."** — Buffett

## Key Metrics Buffett Focuses On

### The Three Statements

| Statement | What to Focus On |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **Income Statement** | Revenue sustainability, gross margin trends, non-recurring items |
| **Balance Sheet** | Debt structure (interest-bearing debt ratio), goodwill reasonableness, receivables/inventory quality |
| **Cash Flow Statement** | Operating cash flow vs. net income, rigidity of CapEx, FCF stability |

### Owner Earnings (Buffett's Custom Metric)

```
Owner Earnings = Net Income
               + Depreciation & Amortization
               - Capital expenditures REQUIRED to maintain competitive position
```

This approximates "what the business truly earned for shareholders."

### The $1 Test

> For every dollar of profit retained by the company, has at least $1 of market value been created?

Long-term, if retained profits don't generate at least equivalent market value growth, management should distribute profits as dividends.

## How to Practice: The Three-Step Beginner's Method

**Step 1: Read one company's annual report (3-5 times)**
Pick the most familiar company in your circle of competence. Download the last 3 years of annual reports. First pass: read only the chairman's letter and business discussion — **ignore the numbers**. Second pass: cross-reference numbers with business discussion. Third pass: calculate the key metrics above yourself.

**Step 2: Cross-industry comparison**
Pick 3-5 companies in the same industry. Compare their gross margins, ROE, FCF patterns. Why does the leader have 5 percentage points higher gross margin than #2? Is that gap widening or narrowing?

**Step 3: Build an "anomaly detector"**
After reading years of reports, you'll develop an intuition for "normal." When a number deviates from the normal pattern — stop and dig deeper. Most financial problems show warning signs before they erupt.

### Advanced: Learn to Spot Accounting Fraud

Buffett suggests watching three things:
1. **Is the accounting policy aggressive?** (compare with industry peers)
2. **Related-party transactions** (profits may be funneled to controlling shareholders)
3. **Auditor changes** (frequent changes = the single biggest red flag)

## Common Misconceptions

- ❌ **You need a CPA to read financial statements** — Buffett says he can't understand "every detail," he just looks for "obvious problems" and "obvious advantages." Like hearing an engine knock without being a mechanic.
- ❌ **Just look at profit growth** — Profit up 30% but receivables up 80% and operating cash flow negative? That "growth" is fake. The income statement lies; the cash flow statement, harder.
- ❌ **Rely on auto-calculated metrics from software** — Automated calculations may use wrong denominators (e.g., counting restricted cash as cash). Understanding the source of each number matters more than relying on tools.
- ❌ **Financial analysis = valuation** — Financial statements tell you what the business **has been**. Valuation requires judging the **future**. Financials are the starting point for valuation, not the endpoint.
