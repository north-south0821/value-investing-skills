# 04 — Circle of Competence

> **"You don't have to be an expert on every company, or even many. You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence. The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundary, however, is vital."**
> — Warren Buffett, 1996 Shareholder Letter

## Core Idea

The **Circle of Competence** is arguably the most underrated yet most important principle in Buffett's framework:

1. **Only invest in what you truly understand**
2. **Be honest with yourself: knowing what you don't know matters more than knowing what you do**

## What Does "Understanding" a Business Mean?

Buffett has a concrete standard:

> **"If I had to write a 500-word essay on where this company will be in ten years, could I do it?"**

### Four Levels of Understanding

| Level | Question | You understand if you can... |
|-------|----------|------------------------------|
| **Business** | How does this company make money? | Explain it in three sentences to a 12-year-old |
| **Competition** | Why do customers choose it over alternatives? Will the moat exist in 5 years? | Name 2-3 things competitors cannot do |
| **Financials** | Are the profits real? What's the return on capital? | Read financial reports and identify key signals |
| **Risk** | What could destroy it? How likely? | List 3-5 genuine threats and assess probability |

### The Honesty Test

If the stock price dropped 50% right after you bought:

- **"Great, I can buy more"** → You probably truly understand the company
- **"Did I miss something? Should I sell?"** → You may be outside your circle

Your instinctive reaction to a price drop is the most honest test of understanding.

## Why Circle of Competence Matters

### 1. No circle = no margin of safety
If you don't know what a company is worth (because you don't understand its business), how can you judge whether the current price offers a margin of safety?

### 2. It lets you stay steady during panic
When markets panic, analysts downgrade, news is all bad — only those who **truly understand the business** have the confidence to buy against the crowd.

### 3. It prevents fatal errors
Investing math is asymmetric:
- Gain 50% → need to gain 50%
- Lose 50% → need to gain **100%** to break even
Investing without understanding = risking irreversible errors.

## Defining Your Circle's Boundary

### Buffett's "Three Don'ts"

1. **Don't invest in businesses you can't understand** — Buffett avoided tech for 30 years until he "understood" Apple's model = consumer brand + ecosystem lock-in.

2. **Don't invest in industries you can't predict 10 years out** — Tech hardware, fashion, social media change too fast. Consumer goods, banking, insurance — models are relatively stable.

3. **Don't invest in companies requiring massive ongoing capital just to maintain competitive position** — Airlines, semiconductor manufacturing — profits must be constantly reinvested; shareholders never see true free cash flow.

### Map Your Personal Circle

Ask yourself honestly:
- **Professional/career experience** — What industries, functions give you unique insight?
- **Consumer experience** — What products/brands do you have genuine preferences for? Why?
- **Financial statements you can read** — Which business models can you verify with financial data?
- **"Absolutely Don't Understand" list** — Write these down and permanently avoid.

## Expanding Your Circle

The circle isn't fixed — but expansion requires **deliberate learning**, not scrolling financial news.

### One Industry at a Time

Pick one industry, spend 3-6 months:
1. Read the 3-5 most important books on that industry
2. Read 5 years of annual/quarterly reports from the industry leader
3. Read industry analysis reports, understand the value chain
4. Talk to people working in that industry

**Per industry, you really only need to deeply understand the 2-3 best companies.**

### Buffett's Expansion Journey

| Era | New Domain | Key Driver |
|-----|-----------|------------|
| 1950s-60s | Insurance | Understanding the value of float |
| 1970s | Consumer brands | See's Candies — learned to pay premium for brand moat |
| 1980s | Media, consumer goods | Coca-Cola, Washington Post |
| 2000s | Railroads, energy | BNSF — understanding infrastructure irreplaceability |
| 2010s | Tech (Apple) | Reframing Apple as consumer brand + ecosystem |

Buffett took 50 years expanding from insurance to tech. Note the pace: **deep understanding of one domain at a time, not chasing hot sectors.**

## Common Misconceptions

- ❌ **"I use the product = I understand the business"** — You may use WeChat daily, but do you understand Tencent's ad monetization? Game licensing regulatory impact? Cloud services competitive landscape? Consumer experience is the **starting point**, not the finish line.
- ❌ **Familiarity = understanding** — Familiar = I know what the company does. Understanding = I can estimate its intrinsic value within a reasonable range. The gap between these two is why many people lose money.
- ❌ **Small circle = can't make money** — Buffett says you only need 20 punch cards in a lifetime (20 major investment decisions). If your circle covers just consumer goods and banking, with 2-3 good companies each — that's enough.
- ❌ **Rushing to expand the circle** — Seeing others profit in new energy, AI, and rushing to "expand your circle." That's not expansion — that's FOMO. FOMO-driven circle expansion almost always ends in losses.
