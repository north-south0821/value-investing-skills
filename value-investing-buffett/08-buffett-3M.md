# 08 — The Buffett 3M Investment Framework

> **"We look for: 1) businesses we understand, 2) favorable long-term economics, 3) able and trustworthy management, and 4) a sensible price."**
> — Warren Buffett, 1992 Shareholder Letter

## Core Idea

The **3M Framework** integrates Buffett's core concepts into a three-dimensional decision filter:

```
Moat → Management → Margin of Safety
```

These three M's form a narrowing funnel:

```
All Public Companies (~60,000+ globally)
    ↓ Moat filter
~500 companies with sustainable competitive advantages
    ↓ Management filter
~100 companies with excellent, shareholder-oriented management
    ↓ Margin of Safety filter
≤20 companies at any given time with price significantly below intrinsic value
    ↓
Your Portfolio (5-10 stocks)
```

The essence: **Better to miss a thousand than to wrongly buy one.**

## Moat — First Filter

Is the competitive advantage durable? Will it exist and be stronger in 10 years?

If the answer isn't "definitely yes and stronger" → **immediately exclude.** Regardless of financials, regardless of price.

### 30-Minute Moat Quick Check

1. Do consumers actively seek this product/service, or only buy on discount?
2. If the company raised prices 5%, would customers leave?
3. How much money and time would a new entrant need to erode its market share?
4. Over the past 5 years, have gross margins and ROIC been stable?

If 3 out of 4 answers are unfavorable → no moat. Exclude.

## Management — Second Filter

Good management can't turn a bad business into a good one (moat is prerequisite), but **bad management can destroy a good business**.

Imagine: a wide-moat company whose management makes stupid acquisitions, awards themselves massive compensation, or engages in aggressive financial engineering — the moat erodes rapidly.

### Four Management Assessment Dimensions

#### 1. Competence
- Over 5 years, did management deliver on what they said they would?
- Is profit growth from industry tailwinds or management skill?
- During industry downturns, how did the company perform relative to peers?

#### 2. Integrity
- Does management candidly discuss mistakes and challenges? (Buffett: "We look for CEOs who admit mistakes faster than they claim credit.")
- Are accounting policies conservative or aggressive?
- Significant related-party transactions? Signs of treating the listed company as a personal ATM?

#### 3. Shareholder Orientation
- Does management think like owners?
- Does retained profit generate at least equivalent market value growth? (The $1 Test)
- Is equity compensation reasonable, or excessively dilutive?

#### 4. Capital Allocation
The most underrated management capability, per Buffett. A CEO's most important decision isn't "how to run operations" — it's "how to deploy the profits."

- Are profits paid as dividends, used for buybacks, reinvested, or spent on acquisitions?
- Are acquisitions priced reasonably? (Most acquisitions destroy shareholder value.)
- Are buybacks done when the stock is cheap or expensive?

### Management Traffic Light

| 🟢 Green | 🟡 Yellow | 🔴 Red |
|---------|----------|--------|
| CEO is a major shareholder | CEO holds very few shares | CEO gets massive pay, holds almost no shares |
| Annual report candidly discusses mistakes | Annual report: only good news | Annual report avoids any bad news |
| Conservative accounting | Conventional accounting | Frequent auditor changes, aggressive accounting |
| Buys back stock when undervalued | Also buys back at high prices | Never buys back, or heavy buybacks at highs |
| Disciplined acquisitions | Occasional acquisitions | Acquires for growth's sake, massive goodwill |

## Margin of Safety — Third Filter

The first two M's address **quality**. The third M addresses **price**.

Even a wide-moat company with excellent management, bought at too high a price, delivers mediocre returns.

### Valuation Is Not a Precise Science

Buffett repeatedly emphasizes: he doesn't calculate DCF in spreadsheets. His method:
1. **Qualitative judgment**: Will this company still exist in 10 years? Will it earn more?
2. **Conservative estimation**: Produce a **reasonable range** for intrinsic value (not a precise number)
3. **Wait for margin of safety**: Only buy when price is at least 30% below the lower bound of that range

> **"It's better to be approximately right than precisely wrong."**

## 3M Decision Flowchart

```
Discover a company
    ↓
[1] Do you understand its business? → No → EXCLUDE
    ↓ Yes
[2] Does it have a moat? → No → EXCLUDE
    ↓ Yes
[3] Will the moat exist in 10 years? → Not sure → EXCLUDE
    ↓ Yes
[4] Management competence + integrity? → Poor → EXCLUDE
    ↓ Good
[5] How does management allocate capital? → Poor → EXCLUDE
    ↓ Good
[6] Does current price offer margin of safety? → No → WAIT
    ↓ Yes (≥30%)
[7] Willing to hold for 10 years? → Not sure → RETHINK
    ↓ Yes
BUY (within appropriate position size)
```

## 3M Weighting by Context

| Context | Moat Weight | Management Weight | Margin of Safety Weight |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|------------------------|
| Exceptional quality (Moutai, Coke) | High | Medium (good business compensates for mediocre mgmt) | Medium (acceptable to have somewhat smaller margin) |
| Average quality | Medium | High (management especially critical) | High (need larger margin) |
| Cyclical industry | Medium | High | Very high (buy at cycle bottom) |
| Turnaround | Low (moat of troubled company may not exist) | Very high (need mgmt to turn things around) | High (high risk needs high compensation) |

## Common Misconceptions

- ❌ **3M is a mechanical scoring system** — 3M is a **thinking framework**, not a scoring template. Assigning 7/10 to management, 8/10 to moat, 6/10 to safety margin and calculating a weighted average is precisely NOT how Buffett operates. Qualitative judgment cannot be pseudo-quantified.
- ❌ **The three M's are equally weighted** — "Buy wonderful companies at fair prices" centers on **Moat first, Price second.** Without a moat, no price is cheap enough.
- ❌ **3M passing means automatic buy** — 3M is necessary, not sufficient. Even when all three pass, consider position sizing (no single stock beyond what you can tolerate permanent loss on), portfolio industry diversification, and personal cash flow needs.
